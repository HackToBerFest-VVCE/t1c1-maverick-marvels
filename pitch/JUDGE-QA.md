# Anticipated judge questions

Rehearse these out loud. The first one is the one you will definitely get.

---

## 1. "Why not just use OpenCV and Tesseract? Why pay for an API?"

**The 45-second answer — memorise the shape, not the words.**

> "Three reasons and one concession.
>
> **Cost:** Gemma 4 on the Gemini API is listed at zero dollars, input and
> output. And the weights are open, so we can run the identical model offline
> for nothing. The expensive-API premise doesn't hold.
>
> **Accuracy where it matters:** on handwriting, Tesseract 5 gets roughly half
> of all characters wrong — a 49.6% character error rate on the standard IAM
> benchmark, against about 1.4% for a modern vision model. And on real printed
> Hindi scans, classical OCR's errors concentrate on *numerals* — sixty-nine of
> a hundred and ninety-seven errors in one 2026 benchmark. Numerals are our
> entire audit.
>
> **It's the wrong task:** OCR gives us characters. We need to know which number
> is days-worked and which is the muster-roll number, on a register layout we've
> never seen, and to refuse when it's illegible. Every panchayat's register is
> different, so templates break.
>
> **The concession:** classical OCR fails visibly; a vision model can fail
> confidently. That's exactly why our AI never computes a number, a human
> confirms every field beside the original crop, and our arithmetic checks run
> independently of the model.
>
> And we measured it on our own documents: Tesseract plus regex got [X]%,
> Gemma got [Y]%."

Have the failure gallery ready on a slide.

## 2. "Isn't the API expensive at scale?"

> "It's free — Google lists Gemma 4 at zero cost on the Gemini API with no paid
> tier. But the real answer is we don't depend on it. Apache 2.0 weights, so
> self-hosting costs us nothing but hardware." *Flip the toggle.*

## 3. "LLMs hallucinate. How can you trust it with someone's wages?"

> "We don't trust it with wages. No LLM computes anything in this system — every
> rupee comes from tested Python with integer paise and explicit rounding. The
> model's only job is reading, a human confirms every field it read, and
> arithmetic consistency checks catch a confidently-wrong reading even when the
> human misses it."

## 4. "Your confidence scores are self-reported by the model. Aren't they made up?"

This is the sharpest version of the objection. Phase 14 is what lets you answer.

> "That's the right question, and it's why we calibrated them rather than
> trusting them. Here's the reliability curve — at confidence above 0.85,
> measured accuracy was 97% across n=412 fields. Below the threshold we abstain.
> And the validators are independent of the model entirely: days times rate has
> to equal the amount, dates have to fall inside the muster period. Those catch
> errors no confidence score would."

## 5. "Thirty synthetic documents isn't a real evaluation."

Concede immediately. Do not defend it.

> "Agreed — it's a floor, not a validation. At n=30, our 100% discrepancy recall
> has a 95% lower bound near 89%, and we say so in the README. The documents are
> synthetic and generated by us, which is a real limitation. The next step is
> real consented documents through a social audit unit."

Judges reward this. The MedGemma rubric explicitly asked entrants how they
calculated their estimates.

## 6. "Why Gemma specifically? Why not Gemini or GPT-4?"

> "Open weights, and it's not a technicality. A State Social Audit Unit handling
> citizen entitlement data can't send it to a third-party API. With Gemma they
> don't have to — same model, their hardware, data never leaves the state. A
> closed model makes this product undeployable for its actual institutional
> user."

## 7. "The amounts are tiny. Who cares about ₹21.60?"

> "That's precisely the mechanism. Individually it's never worth an hour of
> someone's time, so nobody claims it, so it's never paid. In one study's
> sample, compensation owed for central-government delays alone came to
> thirty-nine crore rupees — unacknowledged and unpaid. Our reconciler
> aggregates the small amounts into one number an officer has to answer."

## 8. "Will officials actually accept this?"

> "We're not asking anyone to trust AI. We hand them their own muster roll,
> alongside the worker's photographed paper with a SHA-256 hash taken at
> capture, and arithmetic anyone can redo by hand. Section 63 of the Bharatiya
> Sakshya Adhiniyam now explicitly expects hash values for electronic evidence —
> the Supreme Court called the hash an electronic fingerprint in July."

## 9. "What about privacy? These are identifiable poor households."

> "Identifiers are masked to the last four digits everywhere — logs, UI,
> screenshots. EXIF and GPS are stripped in the browser before upload. The
> evidence packet contains hashes, not biometrics. The NFC card holds eighteen
> bytes: a job card number. And self-hosting means a government unit can run
> this with nothing leaving its own network."

## 10. "VB-G RAM G already has digital attendance. Doesn't that solve fraud?"

> "Digitisation moved the fraud rather than ending it. Rajasthan saw a twelve
> hundred crore case using fake photographs and biometric entries — over five
> thousand officials faced action. Jharkhand's concurrent audit found only
> twenty-five percent of muster-roll workers actually present at worksites. The
> worker's paper is the independent ground truth the digital system can't
> overwrite, and that's the thing nobody was reading."

## 11. "Why not use Benford's law to detect anomalies?"

Pre-empted deliberately — do not build this.

> "We looked at it and rejected it. Benford assumes naturally-occurring
> magnitudes, and these wages are rule-generated — three hundred rupees times
> days — so the first-digit distribution is an artefact of the formula, not of
> behaviour. We use duplicate detection and impossibility checks instead.
> Benford would suit material bills and measurement-book quantities, which is a
> roadmap item."

## 12. "Could you match thumbprints or signatures across muster rolls?"

> "Technically adjacent, and we chose not to. Biometric matching from phone
> photos is unreliable, and a false match would accuse a real worker of fraud.
> The most we'd do is flag visually similar marks for a human to look at — and
> that's a decision for a social audit unit, not for us."

## 13. "What happens when the photo is unreadable?"

> "The model marks the field illegible rather than guessing, the interface asks
> for a retake or manual entry, and no calculation runs on an unconfirmed field.
> We report our abstention rate alongside accuracy, because a system that knows
> when to stop is more useful here than one that's confident everywhere."

## 14. "What's your business model / how does this sustain?"

> "It isn't a business. The institutional user is a State Social Audit Unit,
> which already has a statutory mandate and a budget — Andhra Pradesh ran its
> audits at half a percent of programme spend. Our cost structure is a free
> model or self-hosted open weights. The constraint on social audits is
> evidence-assembly labour, and that's the cost we remove."

## 15. "What's the one thing you'd build next?"

Have a real answer ready. Suggested:

> "Real documents through a partner NGO. Everything we've measured is synthetic,
> and the handwriting distribution in a real Jharkhand register is the thing
> most likely to surprise us. Second would be fine-tuning on IIIT's Indic
> handwriting datasets — the open weights make that possible and nothing else
> about our stack would change."

## The two traps

**Do not overclaim legal admissibility.** You pre-fill Part A of the Section 63
certificate. You cannot provide the Part B expert signature. Say "court-ready
preparation", never "court-admissible".

**Do not say "ghost worker" or "fraud detection".** You detect an absence of
paper evidence. Say "a name on the roll with no paper record behind it". If a
judge uses the word "fraud", you can gently correct — that correction will land
better than anything else you say.
